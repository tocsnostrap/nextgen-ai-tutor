# Research and fact check for the plan

Checked on 2026-11-20. Method: each citation and fact in the plan was searched on the web (primary sources preferred: journal/DOI pages, PubMed, ERIC, author or university repositories, gov.uk, EEF, ICO). Every URL below appeared in a search result during this check; none is written from memory. Where only an abstract or a secondary summary was read rather than the full text, the entry says so.

Verdicts: **SUPPORTED** (the source says what the plan says), **PARTLY** (true in part; wording must be softened or the citation changed), **NOT SUPPORTED**, **COULD NOT VERIFY**.

Summary: 29 items checked. 17 SUPPORTED, 12 PARTLY, 0 NOT SUPPORTED, 0 COULD NOT VERIFY. A few details inside otherwise-verified items could not be confirmed and are flagged in the entry.

A general caution that applies to several SUPPORTED items: the studies behind items 3, 4, 5, 10, 12 and 13 used university or secondary-school students, not children aged 6-12. The findings are sound; applying them to primary-age children is an extrapolation the plan should state.

## Part 1: Research citations

### 1. Rosenshine (2012), Principles of Instruction

- **Citation:** Rosenshine, B. (2012). Principles of instruction: Research-based strategies that all teachers should know. *American Educator*, 36(1), 12-19, 39.
- **URL:** https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5c47c0b3e17ba324ad0c9d7f/t/66738271b93d201c36e3d4c9/1718846070059/POI.pdf (hosted copy of the article; the original is on the American Federation of Teachers site, whose URL did not appear in results)
- **What it found:** Sets out ten principles drawn from cognitive science, studies of master teachers and cognitive supports: daily review, new material in small steps, questioning, models, guided practice, checking understanding, a high success rate, scaffolds, independent practice, weekly and monthly review. It states that the optimal success rate appears to be about 80 percent.
- **Plan's claim:** Daily review, small steps, guided practice, check understanding, aim for about 80% success.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Note: the 80% figure comes from observational studies of effective classroom teachers, not from an experiment that tuned the rate. Describe it as a rule of thumb. The article text was read through secondary summaries that quote it.

### 2. Sweller, cognitive load theory and the worked-example effect

- **Citations:**
  - Sweller, J., & Cooper, G. A. (1985). The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra. *Cognition and Instruction*, 2(1), 59-89.
  - Renkl, A., & Atkinson, R. K. (2003). Structuring the transition from example study to problem solving in cognitive skill acquisition: A cognitive load perspective. *Educational Psychologist*, 38(1), 15-22.
  - Kalyuga, S., Ayres, P., Chandler, P., & Sweller, J. (2003). The expertise reversal effect. *Educational Psychologist*, 38(1), 23-31.
- **URLs:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect (overview with the Sweller & Cooper reference); https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/structuring-the-transition-from-example-study-to-problem-solving--2 (Renkl & Atkinson record); https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf (Kalyuga 2007 review of expertise reversal)
- **What they found:** Sweller & Cooper found that learners who studied worked examples outperformed learners who solved the equivalent problems. Renkl & Atkinson proposed fading, where solution steps are removed one at a time until the learner solves the whole problem. The related experiment (Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill, 2003, *Journal of Educational Psychology*) found fading reliably beat example-problem pairs on near transfer, but not reliably on far transfer. Kalyuga et al. showed the worked-example advantage shrinks and can reverse as learners gain expertise.
- **Plan's claim:** Novices learn better from worked examples that are then faded.
- **Verdict:** PARTLY. The claim is well supported by the literature, but not by "Sweller" alone. Fading is Renkl and Atkinson's contribution, and its measured benefit is for near-transfer problems. The plan needs the correct citations and the expertise-reversal caveat (stop showing worked examples once the child is fluent).

### 3. Roediger & Karpicke (2006)

- **Citation:** Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249-255. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/
- **What it found:** Undergraduates read prose passages, then either restudied or took a free-recall test with no feedback. After 5 minutes the restudy group recalled more; after 2 days and 1 week the tested group recalled substantially more. Restudiers were more confident despite retaining less.
- **Plan's claim:** Retrieval practice beats re-reading for long-term retention.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Notes: participants were university students; re-reading was better on the immediate test, so "long-term" must stay in the claim.

### 4. Cepeda et al. (2006)

- **Citation:** Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin*, 132(3), 354-380. DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/
- **What it found:** Meta-analysis of 839 assessments in 317 experiments. Spaced practice produced better recall than massed practice, and the gap between sessions that maximises retention grows as the retention interval grows.
- **Plan's claim:** Spaced practice beats massed practice.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Notes: the review covers verbal recall tasks, mostly with adults. There is no single best gap; it depends on how long the material must be remembered.

### 5. Rohrer & Taylor (2007)

- **Citation:** Rohrer, D., & Taylor, K. (2007). The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning. *Instructional Science*, 35(6), 481-498. DOI 10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8
- **URL:** https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1767/ (author's university repository; the repository lists a slightly different title for the same paper)
- **What it found:** Two experiments with college students. Spacing practice across sessions and mixing problem types (finding the volume of different solids) both improved performance on a later test, although mixed practice lowered performance during practice. The authors note that the participants were college students and the results may not apply to younger students.
- **Plan's claim:** Interleaving or mixing problem types improves later test performance.
- **Verdict:** PARTLY. The finding is real but rests on small experiments with university students on one topic. Dunlosky et al. (2013, item 6) rated interleaved practice only "moderate utility" because the evidence base was thin. The plan should say interleaving is promising and best supported in mathematics, and that it makes practice feel harder. The exact test scores in the paper were not confirmed in this check.

### 6. Dunlosky et al. (2013)

- **Citation:** Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 14(1), 4-58. DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266
- **URL:** https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/pspi/1529100612453266/
- **What it found:** Review of ten study techniques. High utility: practice testing and distributed practice. Moderate: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice. Low: summarisation, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading.
- **Plan's claim:** Practice testing and distributed practice rated high utility; highlighting and re-reading low.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Ratings were confirmed from the APS and university summaries, not the full monograph.

### 7. Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976)

- **Citation:** Wood, D., Bruner, J. S., & Ross, G. (1976). The role of tutoring in problem solving. *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*, 17(2), 89-100. DOI 10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/932126/
- **What it found:** Introduced the term "scaffolding": a tutor controls the parts of a task that are beyond the learner so the learner can complete what is within reach. Based on one tutor working with 30 children aged 3 to 5 on a wooden block pyramid.
- **Plan's claim:** Scaffolding in tutoring.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED as the origin of the concept. It is a small descriptive study, so cite it for the idea and not as evidence of an effect size.

### 8. Hattie & Timperley (2007); Black & Wiliam (1998)

- **Citations:**
  - Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). The power of feedback. *Review of Educational Research*, 77(1), 81-112. DOI 10.3102/003465430298487
  - Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (1998). Inside the black box: Raising standards through classroom assessment. *Phi Delta Kappan*, 80(2), 139-148. This is the practitioner summary of Black, P., & Wiliam, D. (1998). Assessment and classroom learning. *Assessment in Education*, 5(1), 7-74.
- **URLs:** https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/334554-the-power-of-feedback ; https://people.bath.ac.uk/edspd/Weblinks/MA_Ass/Resources/Using%20assessment%20formatively/Black%20&%20Wiliam%201998%20PDK.pdf
- **What they found:** Hattie & Timperley report an average feedback effect of 0.79 across meta-analyses with very wide variation by type. Their model has three questions (Where am I going? How am I going? Where to next?). Feedback about the task and how to improve is among the most effective; praise and feedback about the self are among the least. Black & Wiliam state that formative assessment experiments typically showed effect sizes of 0.4 to 0.7.
- **Plan's claim:** Feedback should tell the learner what to do next; formative assessment raises attainment.
- **Verdict:** PARTLY.
  - "Tell the learner what to do next" is supported. Add that praise about the child as a person ("you're so clever") is among the least effective kinds of feedback.
  - "Formative assessment raises attainment" overstates the certainty if it leans on the 0.4-0.7 figure. Bennett (2011, *Assessment in Education*) criticised how that range was derived, and Kingston & Nash (2011) estimated a weighted mean effect of about 0.20 to 0.30. A later meta-analysis by Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020) put the feedback effect at d = 0.48, lower than the 0.79 in the 2007 paper. Sources for these: https://people.bath.ac.uk/edspd/Weblinks/MA_Ass/Resources/Using%20assessment%20formatively/Bennett%202011%20AiE.pdf ; https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/pare/article/1424/galley/1375/view ; https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full
  - The "Where to next" question was confirmed through secondary summaries; the 2007 PDF could not be parsed in this check.

### 9. Chi & Wylie (2014), ICAP

- **Citation:** Chi, M. T. H., & Wylie, R. (2014). The ICAP framework: Linking cognitive engagement to active learning outcomes. *Educational Psychologist*, 49(4), 219-243. DOI 10.1080/00461520.2014.965823
- **URL:** https://education.asu.edu/lcl/publications/chi-m-t-h-wylie-r-2014-icap-framework-linking-cognitive-engagement-active-learning
- **What it found:** Proposes four modes of engagement defined by overt behaviour (Interactive, Constructive, Active, Passive) and the hypothesis that learning increases in the order passive, active, constructive, interactive. The paper reviews existing studies that fit the ordering.
- **Plan's claim:** Interactive and constructive activities beat active and passive ones.
- **Verdict:** PARTLY. ICAP is a framework and a hypothesis, supported by the studies the authors reviewed, not an established law. A 2023 critique (https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-023-09817-2) argues that overt behaviour is a weak guide to the cognitive processes that drive learning and that practitioners should not rely on ICAP to choose interventions. Soften "beat" to "are predicted to produce more learning than".

### 10. Bloom (1984); VanLehn (2011)

- **Citations:**
  - Bloom, B. S. (1984). The 2 sigma problem: The search for methods of group instruction as effective as one-to-one tutoring. *Educational Researcher*, 13(6), 4-16. DOI 10.3102/0013189X013006004
  - VanLehn, K. (2011). The relative effectiveness of human tutoring, intelligent tutoring systems, and other tutoring systems. *Educational Psychologist*, 46(4), 197-221. DOI 10.1080/00461520.2011.611369
- **URLs:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0013189X013006004 ; https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764
- **What they found:** Bloom reported, from his students' dissertations, that one-to-one tutoring with mastery learning put the average student about two standard deviations above conventional classes. VanLehn's review found the effect of human tutoring was d = 0.79, much lower than 2.0, and that intelligent tutoring systems reached d = 0.76. His breakdown by granularity gives about 0.31 for answer-based, 0.76 for step-based and 0.40 for substep-based systems, each against no tutoring.
- **Plan's claim:** Human tutoring's effect is smaller than two sigma; step-based computer tutors come close to human tutors (about 0.79 against 0.76).
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Notes: the ERIC abstract confirms 0.79 and 0.76; the step-based label for 0.76 and the 0.31/0.40 figures come from secondary summaries of the paper's tables. The studies VanLehn reviewed are mostly STEM subjects with older students and pre-date LLM tutors, so the result does not show that a chat-based tutor for 6-12 year olds matches a human.

### 11. Corbett & Anderson (1995)

- **Citation:** Corbett, A. T., & Anderson, J. R. (1995). Knowledge tracing: Modeling the acquisition of procedural knowledge. *User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction*, 4(4), 253-278. DOI 10.1007/BF01099821
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01099821
- **What it found:** Describes a model that updates the probability a student has learned each rule after every practice opportunity, used for mastery learning in the ACT Programming Tutor. This model is the basis of what is now called Bayesian knowledge tracing.
- **Plan's claim:** Bayesian knowledge tracing.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Note: the paper calls it "knowledge tracing"; "Bayesian" was added later. Springer's citation block shows 1994 for volume 4, while the article date is 1 January 1995; 1995 is the usual citation.

### 12. Bastani et al. (2024/2025)

- **Citation:** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 122(26), e2422633122. The 2024 SSRN preprint was titled "Generative AI Can Harm Learning".
- **URL:** https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486
- **What it found:** Field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school students in Turkey. During practice, a plain ChatGPT-style interface (GPT Base) raised scores by 48% and a tutor with teacher-designed prompts (GPT Tutor) by 127%. On the later exam without AI, GPT Base students scored 17% worse than controls; GPT Tutor students scored about the same as controls.
- **Plan's claim:** Students with unrestricted GPT did worse later without it; a guard-railed tutor version mitigated this.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Two points to state precisely: the guard-railed tutor removed the harm but did not produce a learning gain over the control group on the unassisted exam, and the participants were high-school students in one school studying maths. Update the citation to the published PNAS title and year.

### 13. Kestin et al. (2025)

- **Citation:** Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., & Ponti, G. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT introducing a novel research-based design in an authentic educational setting. *Scientific Reports*, 15, 17458. DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/
- **What it found:** Randomised crossover trial in a Harvard introductory physics course. Students using a purpose-built AI tutor at home had median learning gains more than double those of students in an active-learning class on the same material, spent less time, and reported more engagement and motivation. The tutor used prompts and step-by-step scaffolds written by the instructors.
- **Plan's claim:** Students learned more in less time with a well-designed AI tutor.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Scope limits the plan must state: Harvard undergraduates, one course, two lessons, immediate post-tests, and a tutor with expert-written content for each lesson. It says nothing directly about children. Sources disagree on the sample size (the Harvard Gazette says 194 students; a summary of the paper gave larger per-group counts), so do not quote an N without checking the paper.

### 14. Deci & Ryan (self-determination theory); Hidi & Renninger (2006)

- **Citations:**
  - Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. *American Psychologist*, 55(1), 68-78. DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68
  - Hidi, S., & Renninger, K. A. (2006). The four-phase model of interest development. *Educational Psychologist*, 41(2), 111-127. DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep4102_4
  - Supporting evidence on choice: Patall, E. A., Cooper, H., & Robinson, J. C. (2008). The effects of choice on intrinsic motivation and related outcomes: A meta-analysis of research findings. *Psychological Bulletin*, 134(2), 270-300.
- **URLs:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392867/ ; https://works.swarthmore.edu/fac-education/12 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18298272/
- **What they found:** Ryan & Deci identify autonomy, competence and relatedness as needs whose satisfaction supports intrinsic motivation and well-being. Hidi & Renninger describe four phases (triggered situational, maintained situational, emerging individual, well-developed individual interest) and argue interest needs external support to develop. Patall et al. (41 studies, children and adults) found choice increased intrinsic motivation, effort and task performance, with the largest effect for instructionally irrelevant choices and diminishing returns after several choices.
- **Plan's claim:** Autonomy/choice and interest support motivation.
- **Verdict:** PARTLY. The direction is right, but the plan cites two theory papers as if they measured an effect. Hidi & Renninger is a descriptive model, not a test of an intervention. Add Patall et al. (2008) as the evidence for choice, and limit the claim: a small number of choices helps; many choices do not help more, and competence and relatedness matter as much as autonomy.

### 15. Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006)

- **Citation:** Kirschner, P. A., Sweller, J., & Clark, R. E. (2006). Why minimal guidance during instruction does not work: An analysis of the failure of constructivist, discovery, problem-based, experiential, and inquiry-based teaching. *Educational Psychologist*, 41(2), 75-86. DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1
- **URL:** https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/why-minimal-guidance-during-instruction-does-not-work-an-analysis/
- **What it found:** A theoretical and narrative review arguing that minimally guided instruction is less effective and less efficient than guided instruction, and that the advantage of guidance recedes only when learners have enough prior knowledge to guide themselves.
- **Plan's claim:** Minimally guided instruction is less effective than explicit instruction for novices.
- **Verdict:** PARTLY. That is what the authors conclude, but the paper is an argument, not a meta-analysis, and it was contested in the same journal (Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn, 2007; Schmidt et al., 2007, *Educational Psychologist*, 42(2)), who argued that well-scaffolded inquiry and problem-based learning are not "minimally guided". Write "Kirschner, Sweller and Clark argue that..." and do not use it to rule out guided inquiry.

### 16. Pashler et al. (2008)

- **Citation:** Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008). Learning styles: Concepts and evidence. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 9(3), 105-119. DOI 10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x
- **URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162104/
- **What it found:** Very few studies used a design able to test whether matching instruction to a learner's style improves learning, and of those, several contradicted the idea. The authors conclude there is no adequate evidence base to justify using learning-style assessments in education. They also say many versions have simply not been tested.
- **Plan's claim:** No adequate evidence for matching teaching to learning styles.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED.

### 17. Barrett et al. (2019)

- **Citation:** Barrett, L. F., Adolphs, R., Marsella, S., Martinez, A. M., & Pollak, S. D. (2019). Emotional expressions reconsidered: Challenges to inferring emotion from human facial movements. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 20(1), 1-68. DOI 10.1177/1529100619832930
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6640856
- **What it found:** Reviewing the evidence, the authors conclude that facial configurations do not express emotion categories with enough reliability, specificity or generalisability across people, contexts and cultures to infer a person's emotional state from the face alone. They warn specifically about applications in education, industry and security.
- **Plan's claim:** Facial movements are unreliable indicators of emotion.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Precise wording: faces carry some information, but not enough to infer emotion reliably from facial movements alone.

### 18. Cooper, Robinson & Patall (2006)

- **Citation:** Cooper, H., Robinson, J. C., & Patall, E. A. (2006). Does homework improve academic achievement? A synthesis of research, 1987-2003. *Review of Educational Research*, 76(1), 1-62.
- **URL:** https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/688435
- **What it found:** Across US studies, the correlation between time on homework and achievement was stronger in grades 7-12 than in K-6; for elementary pupils the correlational studies suggest little or no relationship. The authors suggest homework for young children may serve to build study habits.
- **Plan's claim:** Homework has little measured relationship with achievement in primary/elementary years.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Notes: this is a correlational finding about time spent on homework in US schools. It does not show that well-designed short practice is useless for young children. Elementary-level effect sizes were not read from the full text.

### 19. National Reading Panel (2000); Rose Review (2006); DfE Reading Framework

- **Citations:**
  - National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (2000). *Report of the National Reading Panel. Teaching children to read: An evidence-based assessment of the scientific research literature on reading and its implications for reading instruction* (NIH Publication No. 00-4769).
  - Rose, J. (2006). *Independent review of the teaching of early reading: Final report*. Department for Education and Skills.
  - Department for Education (2021, expanded July 2023). *The reading framework*.
- **URLs:** https://www.nichd.nih.gov/research/supported/nrp ; https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/5551/2/report.pdf ; https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-reading-framework-teaching-the-foundations-of-literacy
- **What they found:** The NRP found that systematic phonics (a planned sequence of letter-sound teaching) produced better reading outcomes than unsystematic or no phonics (overall d = 0.41), with benefits from kindergarten to grade 6 and for struggling readers. It did not find synthetic phonics significantly better than other systematic approaches; a panel member's account says the synthetic effect was somewhat higher but not significantly so (https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/synthetic-phonics-or-systematic-phonics-what-does-research-really-say). A later reanalysis estimated smaller effects (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3024599). The Rose Review recommended systematic synthetic phonics as the best route for the vast majority of young children and the "simple view of reading". The DfE Reading Framework makes systematic synthetic phonics the expected method for beginning readers in England and covers reception to year 9.
- **Plan's claim:** Systematic synthetic phonics.
- **Verdict:** PARTLY. English policy (Rose, DfE) does support systematic synthetic phonics, and the product should follow it for England. But the NRP supports systematic phonics generally and should not be cited as evidence that the synthetic variety is superior. NRP effect-size detail was confirmed from secondary sources, not the report itself.

### 21. IES practice guide on fractions

- **Citation:** Siegler, R., Carpenter, T., Fennell, F., Geary, D., Lewis, J., Okamoto, Y., Thompson, L., & Wray, J. (2010). *Developing effective fractions instruction for kindergarten through 8th grade: A practice guide* (NCEE 2010-4039). Institute of Education Sciences, U.S. Department of Education.
- **URL:** https://kilthub.cmu.edu/articles/journal_contribution/Developing_Effective_Fractions_Instruction_for_Kindergarten_Through_8th_Grade/6614288
- **What it found:** Five recommendations: build on children's informal understanding of sharing and proportionality; teach that fractions are numbers, using number lines as a central tool; explain why computation procedures work; develop understanding of ratio, rate and proportion; and improve teachers' own understanding of fractions. Each recommendation carries an evidence rating.
- **Plan's claim:** Used as the source for fractions teaching.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. The individual evidence ratings were not read; check them before quoting any recommendation as "strong evidence".

### 22. Graham & Perin (2007), Writing Next

- **Citation:** Graham, S., & Perin, D. (2007). *Writing next: Effective strategies to improve writing of adolescents in middle and high schools. A report to Carnegie Corporation of New York*. Alliance for Excellent Education. Academic version: Graham, S., & Perin, D. (2007). A meta-analysis of writing instruction for adolescent students. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 99(3), 445-476.
- **URL:** https://www.carnegie.org/publications/writing-next/
- **What it found:** Meta-analysis identifying eleven elements of effective writing instruction (including strategy instruction, summarisation, collaborative writing, specific product goals, sentence combining and study of models).
- **Age range actually covered:** US grades 4 to 12, roughly ages 9 to 18. The report is framed around adolescents in middle and high school.
- **Plan's claim:** Used as the evidence base for writing at ages 9-12.
- **Verdict:** PARTLY. Ages 9-12 fall inside the range reviewed (grades 4-7), so the use is defensible, but the report is about adolescents, the evidence is weighted towards older pupils, and it says nothing about ages 6-8. State the grade range and add a primary-age source such as the EEF's *Improving Literacy in Key Stage 2*.

The licence does not cover departmental logos and crests, third-party rights the provider cannot license, or personal data, and it gives no right to suggest official status or endorsement.
- **Plan's claim:** The programmes of study are Crown copyright under OGL v3.0 and may be reused in a commercial or private product.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED, with three conditions the plan should record: include the OGL attribution; do not use DfE or government logos; do not imply DfE endorsement. Also note that a Commons Library briefing found in this check says a revised national curriculum is due to be published in 2027 for first teaching in 2028 (https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/id/eprint/41839/1/SN06798.pdf), so content mapped to the 2013-14 programmes of study will need revisiting. That timetable was seen only in a search summary and not checked against gov.uk.

### C. Home education law in England

- **Sources:** https://www.gov.uk/home-education ; DfE *Elective home education: departmental guidance for parents* (April 2019): https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5ca21e22e5274a77d9d26feb/EHE_guidance_for_parentsafterconsultationv2.2.pdf ; guidance landing page: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/elective-home-education ; Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2026/21/body
- **What was found:** Education Act 1996, section 7: "The parent of every child of compulsory school age shall cause him to receive efficient full-time education suitable (a) to his age, ability and aptitude, and (b) to any special educational needs he may have, either by regular attendance at school or otherwise." The gov.uk page says a child must receive full-time education from age 5 and that parents do not have to follow the national curriculum. The 2019 DfE guidance (para 2.11) says parents are not required to teach the National Curriculum or provide a broad and balanced curriculum. There is no legal definition of "full-time" or "suitable" beyond section 7. The DfE guidance is non-statutory.
- **Plan's claim:** Home education is legal, parents need not follow the National Curriculum, and the parent is responsible for a suitable full-time education.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. One change since the plan's sources: the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 29 April 2026. It keeps home education lawful but provides for local authority registers of children not in school and for local authority consent before some children are withdrawn from school. Sources found here disagree on when those provisions start, and this check did not confirm a commencement date. The plan should mention the Act and avoid saying parents have no duty to notify anyone.

### D. Voice data under UK GDPR

- **Sources:** ICO biometric recognition guidance: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/biometric-data-guidance-biometric-recognition/biometric-recognition/ ; https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/biometric-data-guidance-biometric-recognition/key-data-protection-concepts/ ; https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-is-special-category-data/
- **What was found:** Personal data is biometric data when it results from specific technical processing of physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics and allows or confirms unique identification. Voice recognition is listed as a biometric technique. Biometric data is special category data only when used for the purpose of uniquely identifying someone; the attempt does not have to succeed. A plain audio recording is not biometric data until voice features are extracted from it. Special category processing needs an Article 9 condition, and the ICO says explicit consent is likely to be the most appropriate one in many cases.
- **Plan's claim:** Voice data used to uniquely identify a person is biometric / special category data.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. For the design: speech-to-text on a child's audio without voiceprints or speaker identification is not special category biometric processing, but the audio is still a child's personal data. Any speaker recognition feature ("which child is speaking") would be special category. The ICO pages were read through search summaries of cached copies; confirm wording on the live pages before relying on it in a DPIA.

### F. Languages at Key Stage 2

- **Sources:** https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-languages-progammes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-languages-progammes-of-study ; https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-framework-for-key-stages-1-to-4/the-national-curriculum-in-england-framework-for-key-stages-1-to-4 ; British Council *Language Trends England 2025*: https://www.britishcouncil.org/research-insight/language-trends-england-2025 ; 2026 report: https://www.britishcouncil.org/sites/default/files/language_trends_england_report_2026.pdf
- **What was found:** A foreign language is a statutory national curriculum subject at key stage 2 (years 3-6) and not at key stage 1. - **Plan's claim:** Foreign languages are statutory at KS2; French and Spanish are the most commonly taught.
- **Verdict:** SUPPORTED. Notes: the national curriculum binds maintained schools, not academies or home educators. The Spanish and German percentages and the 2026 figures come from secondary summaries; the 2026 report page returned an error during this check, so read the PDF before quoting 2026 numbers.
